## Sections 3 and 4 — Passages and questions

Original-first form: original in Passage 1, AI rewrite in Passage 2. AI-first form: AI rewrite in Passage 1, original in Passage 2. After each passage, ask:

1. How clear and easy to understand is this passage? — 1 Not at all to 7 Extremely.
2. How engaging is this passage? — 1 Not at all to 7 Extremely.
3. Overall, how well written is this passage? — 1 Not at all to 7 Extremely.
4. According to the passage, about how often do sleep cycles repeat? — 30 minutes; 60 minutes; **90 minutes**; 120 minutes.

All four questions are required after each passage.

### Original NIH passage

When you learn something new, the best way to remember it is to sleep on it. That’s because sleeping helps strengthen memories you’ve formed throughout the day. It also helps to link new memories to earlier ones. You might even come up with creative new ideas while you slumber.

What happens to memories in your brain while you sleep? And how does lack of sleep affect your ability to learn and remember? NIH-funded scientists have been gathering clues about the complex relationship between sleep and memory. Their findings might eventually lead to new approaches to help students learn or help older people hold onto memories as they age.

“We’ve learned that sleep before learning helps prepare your brain for initial formation of memories,” says Dr. Matthew Walker, a sleep scientist at the University of California, Berkeley. “And then, sleep after learning is essential to help save and cement that new information into the architecture of the brain, meaning that you’re less likely to forget it.”

While you snooze, your brain cycles through different phases of sleep, including light sleep, deep sleep, and rapid eye movement (REM) sleep, when dreaming often occurs. The cycles repeat about every 90 minutes”

### AI rewrite

For something you’ve just learned to stay with you, your best bet is to get some sleep. As you rest, memories from your day grow stronger and become connected with older ones. Your sleeping mind may also put together creative ideas you hadn’t thought of before.

Better ways to help students learn and older adults retain memories as they age may eventually emerge from research funded by NIH. Scientists are piecing together the complicated connection between sleep and memory, examining what happens to memories during sleep and how going without it affects your capacity to learn and remember.

“We’ve learned that sleep before learning helps prepare your brain for initial formation of memories,” says Dr. Matthew Walker, a sleep scientist at the University of California, Berkeley. “And then, sleep after learning is essential to help save and cement that new information into the architecture of the brain, meaning that you’re less likely to forget it.”

Dreaming often takes place in rapid eye movement (REM) sleep, one of several stages your brain passes through as you rest. Light and deep sleep are also part of this recurring pattern, which takes about 90 minutes to complete.
