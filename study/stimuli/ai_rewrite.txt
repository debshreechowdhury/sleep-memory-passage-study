For something you’ve just learned to stay with you, your best bet is to get some sleep. As you rest, memories from your day grow stronger and become connected with older ones. Your sleeping mind may also put together creative ideas you hadn’t thought of before.

Better ways to help students learn and older adults retain memories as they age may eventually emerge from research funded by NIH. Scientists are piecing together the complicated connection between sleep and memory, examining what happens to memories during sleep and how going without it affects your capacity to learn and remember.

“We’ve learned that sleep before learning helps prepare your brain for initial formation of memories,” says Dr. Matthew Walker, a sleep scientist at the University of California, Berkeley. “And then, sleep after learning is essential to help save and cement that new information into the architecture of the brain, meaning that you’re less likely to forget it.”

Dreaming often takes place in rapid eye movement (REM) sleep, one of several stages your brain passes through as you rest. Light and deep sleep are also part of this recurring pattern, which takes about 90 minutes to complete.
